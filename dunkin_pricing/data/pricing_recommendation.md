# Dunkin' Campus Pricing Recommendation (Provisional)

_Hypothesis-generation tool for the 9/24 preliminary market analysis - not a measured elasticity estimate. Re-run or validate against real regression results once survey data lands after 10/19._

## Scenarios considered
### 25-cent discount vs off-campus
- Hot coffee: $2.75 | Latte: $5.00
- Expected relative appeal: **medium**
- Rationale: Hot coffee at $2.75 undercuts all on‑campus competitors ($3.00–$3.25), so for drip/regular coffee this is clearly the lowest posted price and should be attractive to price-sensitive students who care about small but visible savings on frequent purchases. Lattes at $5.00 match Peet’s and Brew and undercut Starbucks’ $5.75, so Dunkin’ is never more expensive and sometimes cheaper, but not meaningfully cheaper than the closest substitutes. Overall, price-sensitive students will likely view Dunkin’ as the value option for basic coffee and a parity-priced option for espresso drinks, but the discount magnitudes (25–50 cents) are modest, so price alone may not be strong enough to drive switching unless students are already positively inclined toward Dunkin’ or are very frequent coffee buyers. The unresolved question about accepting dining dollars/meal plans is critical: if Dunkin’ cannot accept them, then even a modest cash price advantage is likely to be outweighed for many students by the friction of paying out-of-pocket; if Dunkin’ can accept them, then the low-price positioning on drip coffee and parity on lattes becomes much more compelling relative to incumbents.
- Survey must confirm: Whether a majority of price-sensitive students (especially those on meal plans) perceive a 25–50 cent per-cup savings on drip coffee versus on-campus Starbucks/Peet’s/Brew as meaningful enough to change their regular purchase location, assuming Dunkin’ can accept dining dollars/meal-plan payments just like the incumbents.

### Match off-campus price
- Hot coffee: $3.00 | Latte: $5.25
- Expected relative appeal: **medium**
- Rationale: At $3.00 for hot coffee, Dunkin’ exactly matches Peet’s and Brew and undercuts Starbucks by $0.25, so its brewed coffee offer is competitively positioned but not uniquely cheaper. For lattes at $5.25, Dunkin’ is cheaper than Starbucks ($5.75) but more expensive than Peet’s and Brew ($5.00), putting it in the middle of the pack. For highly price-sensitive students comparing only on-campus options, Dunkin’ is unlikely to dominate purely on price: it ties the low price on coffee but is slightly worse than the low-price incumbents on lattes, while offering no clear price advantage over the cheapest options. Its overall price image will likely be “competitive but not the cheapest,” which should yield moderate relative appeal on price alone, with brand preference and payment convenience becoming the decisive factors rather than the posted prices themselves.
- Survey must confirm: That a $0.25 premium on lattes versus the lowest-priced on-campus options (Peet’s, Brew) is small enough that most price-sensitive students either ignore it or trade it off in favor of Dunkin’s brand and location. The student survey must explicitly measure: (1) whether students notice and care about $0.25 drink price differences in daily coffee purchases; (2) how many would switch from their current on-campus coffee spot to Dunkin’ at equal hot-coffee and slightly higher latte prices; and (3) how strongly price, versus brand and payment method (dining dollars/meal plan), drives their choice among on-campus coffee options.

### 25-cent premium vs off-campus
- Hot coffee: $3.25 | Latte: $5.50
- Expected relative appeal: **medium**
- Rationale: For hot coffee, Dunkin’ matches Starbucks’ $3.25 and sits at a $0.25 premium versus Peet’s and Brew at $3.00. For lattes, Dunkin’ undercuts Starbucks ($5.50 vs $5.75) but carries a $0.50 premium to Peet’s/Brew ($5.00). To a price-sensitive student who is largely focused on minimizing spend among on-campus options, Dunkin’ is not the lowest-cost choice for any drink, but it is also not the most expensive overall: it’s tied for highest on drip coffee and sits between the premium Starbucks and value Peet’s/Brew for lattes. That creates a mid-pack price position: clearly weaker on pure price vs Peet’s/Brew, slightly stronger vs Starbucks for lattes, and neutral vs Starbucks for hot coffee. The prior 4.2/5 utility suggests there is meaningful brand-driven willingness to consider Dunkin’, so this mid-tier price positioning is unlikely to be a deal-breaker, but it also does not offer a clear savings incentive relative to the cheaper incumbents. Net: price alone likely yields moderate appeal—neither bargain nor premium gouge—conditional on Dunkin’ being integrated on-campus similarly to others.
- Survey must confirm: That, holding payment convenience equal (i.e., Dunkin’ fully accepts dining dollars/meal plan), students’ brand preference for Dunkin’ is strong enough that a $0.25 premium on hot coffee and $0.50 premium on lattes versus Peet’s/Brew does not materially reduce their choice probability, and that a majority of price-sensitive students view these small premiums as negligible compared to total daily/weekly coffee spend. The survey must measure (a) stated willingness to choose Dunkin’ vs Peet’s/Brew at these exact price gaps, under identical payment options, and (b) what proportion of students treat $0.25–$0.50 per drink as a meaningful price difference when deciding where to buy coffee on campus.

## Recommendation: Match off-campus price: hot coffee $3.00, latte $5.25
Provisional recommendation: Launch the campus Dunkin’ at its standard off‑campus prices ($3.00 hot coffee, $5.25 latte). This positions Dunkin’ as competitively priced but not deeply discounted, protecting unit economics in a high-rent, seasonally volatile campus environment while still undercutting Starbucks on both drip and espresso and matching Peet’s/Brew on core drip coffee. It monetizes the already-validated 4.2/5 brand utility and ‘grab‑and‑go’ niche without training students to expect a permanent discount that may be hard to sustain on a revenue‑share campus lease. This recommendation is explicitly provisional and must be validated (or revised) after collecting student survey data on price sensitivity, perceived value, and payment methods.

**Supporting points:**
- Protects margins under campus-specific cost pressures: Campus locations often carry premium rent and/or revenue-share with the university and also suffer from academic-calendar downtime (summer, long breaks). Matching existing off‑campus prices avoids locking the franchisee into a structurally thinner margin that a $0.25 discount on every cup would create in an already compressed operating environment.
- Still offers a clear, defensible value story vs Starbucks: At $3.00 drip and $5.25 lattes, Dunkin’ undercuts on-campus Starbucks ($3.25 drip, ~$5.75 latte) while offering a different, speed/value-oriented brand. This makes Dunkin’ the cheaper ‘grab-and-go’ chain alternative to the incumbent premium brand, leveraging previously measured 4.2/5 utility without racing to the bottom on price.
- Competitive—but not sacrificial—position vs Peet’s/Brew: For drip, Dunkin’ ties the low-end incumbents ($3.00), so price-sensitive basic-coffee buyers won’t see Dunkin’ as more expensive. Lattes at $5.25 carry only a small $0.25 premium to Peet’s/Brew ($5.00), which is likely to be behaviorally small relative to total student spending, especially when offset by Dunkin’s brand familiarity and faster-service positioning.
- Aligns with brand and daypart strengths rather than pure price warfare: The known perk is Dunkin’s differentiated ‘quick-service, donut-and-coffee’ identity vs Starbucks’ sit‑down ‘third place.’ Matching off‑campus prices keeps Dunkin’ clearly in a value, high-throughput position without needing extreme discounting; traffic can be driven by location, speed, and habit transfer from Dunkin’ regions, not just a 25–50 cent headline gap.
- Avoids creating a discount anchor that’s hard to move later: Launching at a 25-cent discount would teach students that on-campus Dunkin’ is cheaper than “real” Dunkin’. If campus lease or wage pressures tighten, moving prices up later risks perception damage. By starting at parity with off‑campus, Dunkin’ retains future flexibility to run targeted promotions (finals-week deals, meal-plan bundles) rather than embedding a permanent structural discount.
- Balances cannibalization risk and incremental volume: Deep discounts (Scenario 1) might simply shift existing spend from Peet’s/Brew/Starbucks to Dunkin’ at lower margins, not increase overall campus coffee consumption. A “competitive but not cheapest” posture reduces the likelihood that Dunkin’ overpays (via discounting) for share that would have come anyway due to brand loyalty transfer and convenient walkable location.
- Operational simplicity and brand consistency for franchisees: Keeping campus pricing aligned with off‑campus norms simplifies menu boards, marketing, training, and customer expectations, especially for students who already know Dunkin’s reference prices from home markets. That consistency matters when franchise economics are already more complex due to university contracts and seasonal demand swings.

**Risks to watch:**
- If Dunkin’ cannot accept dining dollars/meal-plan payments, even a ‘competitive’ price will likely be insufficient; students may strongly prefer prepaid options at Starbucks/Peet’s/Brew, making cash/card-only Dunkin’ a niche choice despite attractive posted prices.
- Highly price-sensitive latte buyers may notice the $0.25 premium vs Peet’s/Brew and avoid Dunkin’ for espresso drinks, capping share in a high-margin category and limiting total average ticket even if drip coffee performs well.
- The “competitive but not cheapest” positioning may fail to cut through in a crowded, three-competitor environment if Dunkin’s brand appeal on this specific campus is weaker than the 4.2/5 utility suggests or if students see Starbucks as the social default and Peet’s/Brew as the value default.
- If campus lease terms (rent or revenue share) are harsher than expected or labor costs are unusually high, even standard off‑campus pricing could be too low to hit franchisee return thresholds, forcing early price increases and possible backlash.
- There is a risk that launching without a visible discount yields slower initial trial than a more aggressive value offer; in a fixed student population, slow ramp-up may delay the point at which the store reaches sustainable volumes during the academic year.

**What the survey must confirm:**
- Do students actually notice and care about $0.25 price differences on drip and $0.25–$0.50 on lattes when choosing among on-campus coffee options, or are factors like brand, line length, and location more decisive? The survey should quantify willingness to switch for these exact price gaps.
- How many students are on meal plans, what proportion of their coffee spend currently flows through dining dollars/campus cash, and how strongly they prioritize using prepaid funds over saving $0.25–$0.50 per drink. This needs to be tested under scenarios where Dunkin’ both can and cannot accept dining payments.
- Relative brand and experience preference among Starbucks, Peet’s, Brew, and Dunkin’: given equal drip price and a $0.25 latte premium vs Peet’s/Brew, what share of students state they would choose Dunkin’ most of the time, occasionally, or rarely? This must be measured separately for drip vs espresso buyers.
- Perceived value of Dunkin’ at matched off‑campus prices: do students see $3.00/$5.25 as ‘fair’ or ‘good value’ on campus, or do they expect a campus discount because of the grab‑and‑go, QSR positioning? The survey should test value perceptions at all three proposed price sets.
- Elasticity by segment and daypart: among heavy daily users, latte vs drip buyers, and ‘between classes’ vs ‘sit and study’ occasions, how responsive is store choice to the specific price points under consideration? This will validate whether the medium-appeal expectation for the match-price scenario is realistic or needs to be revised.
